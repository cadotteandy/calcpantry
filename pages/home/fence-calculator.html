How the fence calculator works

A wood fence is a series of identical sections: two posts, two or three horizontal rails between them, and boards or pickets nailed to the rails. The calculator divides the fence length by the post spacing to find the number of sections, then counts the materials each section needs.

  • Posts: one per section plus one to close the run, plus one extra for each gate so the gate has its own hinge and latch posts. Corners are not counted automatically; add one post per corner.
  • Rails: 2 per section for fences 5 ft and under, 3 per section for 6 and 8 ft fences.
  • Boards: the fence length in inches divided by board width plus gap, plus 5% for split, warped or knotted boards you'll reject. Gates are covered with the same boards, so gate width isn't subtracted.
  • Concrete: bags per post × posts. Two 50 lb bags suit a 4×4 post in an 8–10 in hole about 2 ft deep; use 3 for deeper holes or gate posts.

The formula

sections = fence length ÷ post spacing, rounded up
posts = sections + 1 + gates
rails = sections × (2 if height ≤ 5 ft, otherwise 3)
boards = fence length × 12 ÷ (board width + gap) × 1.05, rounded up
concrete bags = posts × bags per post

Tips before you dig

  • Call 811 first. It's free, and required in most states, to have buried utility lines marked a few days before you dig post holes.
  • Check the rules. Many towns and HOAs limit fence height (often 4 ft in front yards and 6 ft in back), require a permit and set how far from the property line the fence must sit.
  • Use ground-contact posts. Buy posts rated for ground contact (UC4A or better). Crown the concrete at the top so water runs away from the post.
  • Set the end and gate posts first, run a string line between them, and set line posts to the string.
  • Leave a gap at the bottom. Keep boards about 2 in above the soil so they don't wick moisture and rot.
  • Plan for fasteners. Use exterior-rated nails or screws: about 4 per board on a 2-rail fence and 6 on a 3-rail fence.
