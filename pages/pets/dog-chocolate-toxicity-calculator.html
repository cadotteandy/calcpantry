Why chocolate is dangerous for dogs

Chocolate contains two related stimulants, theobromine and caffeine, known together as methylxanthines. Dogs break down theobromine far more slowly than people do, so it builds up and overstimulates the heart and nervous system. The darker and less sweet the chocolate, the more of these compounds it contains per ounce. That is why a few squares of baking chocolate can be more dangerous than a whole milk chocolate bar.

This calculator follows the approach in the Merck Veterinary Manual: add up the total methylxanthines eaten, divide by the dog's weight in kilograms, and compare the dose with published thresholds. It is a rough guide to help you describe the situation to a vet, not a diagnosis.

The formula

dog weight in kg = weight in lb × 0.4536
amount in oz = grams ÷ 28.35
total mg = amount in oz × mg per oz for that chocolate
dose (mg/kg) = total mg ÷ dog weight in kg

What to do right now

  • Call first. Your vet, an emergency clinic, ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 or the Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661. Both hotlines are staffed 24/7 and charge a consultation fee.
  • Have details ready: your dog's weight, the product (keep the wrapper), how much is missing and roughly when it was eaten.
  • Don't induce vomiting at home unless a vet specifically tells you to. Home remedies such as salt or hydrogen peroxide can cause serious harm, and vets use safer drugs.
  • Don't wait for symptoms. Signs often take hours to appear, and treatment is simpler and more effective early.
  • Watch for other hazards in the same treat: raisins, macadamia nuts, xylitol (a sweetener in some sugar-free products) and wrappers can all cause separate problems.

Some dogs are more sensitive than the thresholds suggest, including dogs with heart disease, seniors and very small dogs, and a high-fat binge can trigger pancreatitis even when theobromine is low.
