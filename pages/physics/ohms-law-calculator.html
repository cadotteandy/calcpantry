How Ohm's law works

Voltage is the electrical "push," current is the flow of charge, and resistance is how strongly a component resists that flow. Ohm's law ties the three together: for a given resistance, doubling the voltage doubles the current.

Power, measured in watts, is how much energy the component uses or turns into heat each second. Because power depends on voltage and current, and Ohm's law links those to resistance, knowing any two of the four values lets you find the other two.

The formula

V = I × R    I = V ÷ R    R = V ÷ I
P = V × I    P = I² × R    P = V² ÷ R
I = √(P ÷ R)    V = √(P × R)

Work in base units (volts, amps, ohms, watts), then add prefixes back at the end. The calculator does both steps for you.

Example: a 240 Ω resistor on 12 V

You connect a 240 Ω resistor across a 12 V supply (the default values).

StepResult
Current: I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 2400.05 A = 50 mA
Power: P = V² ÷ R = 144 ÷ 2400.6 W = 600 mW
Check: P = V × I = 12 × 0.050.6 W

A standard 1/4 W (250 mW) resistor would run far too hot here. Choose one rated 1 W or more.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting prefixes. 12 V ÷ 240 kΩ is 50 µA, not 50 mA. Convert kΩ and mA to base units before dividing.
  • Using Ohm's law on an LED. LEDs have a nearly fixed voltage drop. For a series resistor, use R = (supply voltage − LED voltage) ÷ current.
  • Ignoring power ratings. The right resistance can still burn out if its wattage rating is too low.
  • Mixing up AC and DC. For mains AC, use RMS voltage and current (like 120 V); the formulas then work for resistive loads such as heaters.
