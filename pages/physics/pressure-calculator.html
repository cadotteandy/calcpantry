What pressure is

Pressure is how concentrated a force is. The same push spread over a large area makes a low pressure; squeezed onto a small area it makes a high one. That's why you can lie on a bed of many nails but not stand on one, and why snowshoes, tank treads and wide tires spread weight out.

The SI unit is the pascal (Pa), one newton per square meter. Because a pascal is tiny, everyday pressures are usually given in kilopascals (kPa), bar, atmospheres (atm) or, in the US, pounds per square inch (psi).

The formula

P = F ÷ A
F = P × A
A = F ÷ P
1 atm = 101,325 Pa    1 bar = 100,000 Pa    1 psi = 6,894.757 Pa

Convert areas carefully: 1 cm² = 0.0001 m², 1 mm² = 0.000001 m², 1 in² = 0.00064516 m² and 1 ft² = 0.09290304 m². Squaring the unit squares the conversion factor, which is the most common source of mistakes.

Example: 1,000 N on 25 cm²

A force of 1,000 N (roughly the weight of a 102 kg mass) presses on an area of 25 cm² (the default values).

Common mistakes

  • Converting area like length. 1 cm = 0.01 m, but 1 cm² = 0.0001 m². Forgetting to square gives answers 100 times too small.
  • Using mass instead of force. A 70 kg person presses with about 686 N (70 × 9.80665), not 70 N.
  • Mixing gauge and absolute pressure. Tire and pump gauges read pressure above the air around them. Add 101.325 kPa (14.7 psi) for absolute pressure.
  • Using the whole object's area. Only the contact area counts: the footprint of a tire, not its whole surface.
