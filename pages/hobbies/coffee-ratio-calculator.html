How coffee ratios work

A brew ratio is the weight of coffee to the weight of water. At 1:16 you use 1 gram of coffee for every 16 grams of water. Because 1 ml of water weighs 1 gram, you can read water in milliliters or grams interchangeably. Weighing is the single biggest step toward consistent coffee: scoops and tablespoons vary with grind size and roast by 20% or more.

The ratio controls strength. A lower number (1:12) gives a stronger, heavier cup; a higher number (1:18) gives a lighter one. Grind size and brew time control extraction, which is why each method comes with its own grind suggestion.

The formula

coffee (g) = water (g) ÷ ratio
tablespoons ≈ coffee (g) ÷ 5.5
brewed coffee ≈ water − 2 × coffee
water for a target volume = target × ratio ÷ (ratio − 2)

The last line is used in cups mode. Wet grounds keep about twice their dry weight in water, so to end up with two full 8 oz mugs you need to start with a bit more than 16 ounces.

These are common starting points drawn from roaster brew guides, not rules. The Specialty Coffee Association's Golden Cup standard, about 55 g per liter (roughly 1:18), sits at the light end of the filter range.

Dialing it in

  • Change one thing at a time. Fix the ratio first, then adjust grind until it tastes right.
  • Sour or thin? Grind finer or brew longer. Bitter or harsh? Grind coarser or shorten the brew.
  • Too strong or too weak? That's the ratio. Move it by one step (1:15 to 1:16) and taste again.
  • Light roasts are denser and often taste better a little stronger, around 1:15.
