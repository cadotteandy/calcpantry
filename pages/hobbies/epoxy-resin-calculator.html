How much epoxy resin you need

Epoxy is sold by volume, so the job is to work out the volume of the space you're filling. For a flat coat that's the surface area times the pour depth. For coasters it's the area of a circle times the depth, times the number of pieces. For a river table only the channel between the slabs gets filled, so you use the river's length times its average width, or a share of the whole table area if you've sketched it out that way. The calculator converts cubic inches to fluid ounces, adds a waste allowance, and splits the total by your kit's mix ratio.

The formula

rectangle volume = length × width × depth (cu in)
circle volume = π × (diameter ÷ 2)² × depth × pieces
river volume = length × average river width × depth
fluid ounces = cubic inches ÷ 1.805
total to mix = fluid ounces × (1 + waste %)
part A = total × A ÷ (A + B), part B = total − part A

The 1.805 comes from the US gallon: 231 cubic inches divided by 128 fluid ounces. Mix ratios here are by volume, which is how most hobby kits are labeled. If your kit gives a ratio by weight, measure on a scale using the label's numbers, because resin and hardener rarely have the same density.

Pour depth: coating resin vs deep-pour resin

Epoxy gives off heat as it cures, and a thick mass traps that heat. Too much in one pour can yellow, crack, ripple, trap bubbles or even smoke. Manufacturers design products around a maximum layer depth:

Resin typeTypical mixMax per layer
Tabletop / coating1:1About 1/8 in
Art resin1:1About 1/4 in
Deep pour / casting2:1 or 3:11–4 in, depending on product

The "pours at label limits" line assumes 1/8 inch per tabletop coat and 2 inches per deep-pour layer. Your label wins, especially for deep-pour resins, where some allow only 1 inch and others 4 inches. Wait for each layer to cool and firm up as directed before pouring the next.

Tips for estimating accurately

  • Measure a river channel in several places. Live edges wander. Measure the width every foot or so and average the numbers, and use the deepest point of the mold for depth.
  • Seal the wood first. Bare wood absorbs resin and releases air bubbles. A thin seal coat, worked in with a brush or spreader, uses roughly a quarter of what a flood coat does.
  • Raise waste for river tables. Bark pockets, cracks and a mold that may seep easily eat 15–20% extra. Running short partway through a deep pour is far worse than having leftover resin.
  • Mix in batches you can pour in time. Working time drops as the batch gets bigger. Deep-pour resins give you longer, but check the label's maximum batch size too.
  • Work at room temperature. Most resins want about 70–80°F for pouring and curing. Cold resin is thick and holds bubbles.
