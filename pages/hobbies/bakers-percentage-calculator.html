How baker's percentages work

Bakers write recipes with flour as 100% and every other ingredient as a percentage of the flour weight. A dough with 62% hydration has 62 grams of water for every 100 grams of flour, no matter how big the batch. That makes a formula easy to scale, easy to compare with other recipes, and easy to adjust one thing at a time. The percentages add up to more than 100; that's expected.

This calculator works both ways. Start from a flour weight to get each ingredient and the total dough. Or, for pizza, start from how many dough balls you want and how heavy each one should be, and it works backward to the flour.

The formula

ingredient (g) = flour (g) × ingredient % ÷ 100
total % = 100 + water % + salt % + yeast % + starter % + oil % + sugar %
flour (g) = balls × ball weight ÷ (total % ÷ 100)
starter flour = starter ÷ (1 + starter hydration); starter water = starter − starter flour
effective hydration = (water + starter water) ÷ (flour + starter flour)

Yeast is entered as instant dry yeast. If you use active dry, the calculator multiplies by 1.25; for fresh (cake) yeast, by 3. Those are the usual substitution rules; exact strength varies by brand.

Four 250 g dough balls with the Neapolitan preset: 62% water, 3% salt, 0.2% instant yeast. The total is 165.2%, so the flour is 1,000 ÷ 1.652.

These are common starting points from pizza and bread formulas, not fixed rules. Neapolitan dough usually sits at 58–65% water and 2.5–3% salt with a very small amount of yeast for a long ferment. New York style uses bread flour at 60–65% hydration with a little oil and sugar to help browning in a home oven. The sourdough preset works out to about 74.5% effective hydration once the starter's water and flour are counted.

Tips

  • Use a scale that reads 0.1 g for yeast and salt. At pizza quantities, a gram of yeast is the difference between a 24-hour and an 8-hour dough.
  • Adjust yeast for time and temperature. Less yeast for long cold ferments, more for same-day dough. Change the yeast percentage, not the rest of the formula.
  • Raise hydration gradually. Flours absorb water differently. Go up 2% at a time and hold back some water until you see how the dough feels.
  • Count all the flour. When a recipe says "20% starter," check whether its percentages include the starter's flour. This calculator doesn't; the effective hydration line shows the full picture.
