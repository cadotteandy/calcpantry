How print size is worked out from pixels

A digital photo is a grid of pixels. Printing spreads that grid across paper, and print resolution says how densely: 300 DPI (strictly, pixels per inch) puts 300 pixels in every inch. So the biggest print a file supports at a given quality is just its pixel dimensions divided by the resolution. Print bigger than that and each pixel covers more paper, so fine detail softens.

How much resolution you need depends on viewing distance. A 4×6 held at arm's length shows every flaw, so labs recommend 300 DPI. Around 240 DPI is hard to tell apart on most printers. Wall prints are seen from several feet away, so 150 to 200 DPI usually looks sharp on posters and canvas.

The formulas

max print width (in) = pixel width ÷ DPI
max print height (in) = pixel height ÷ DPI  ·  cm = in × 2.54
megapixels = width × height ÷ 1,000,000
effective DPI for a print = min(long px ÷ long in, short px ÷ short in)
crop = 1 − (print area × effective DPI²) ÷ image pixels

Example: a 12 megapixel phone photo

A 4000 × 3000 pixel image (4:3, 12.0 MP) prints up to 13.3 × 10 inches (33.9 × 25.4 cm) at 300 DPI. Here's how it fares at standard sizes, filling the paper:

The 16×20 at 188 DPI is fine framed on a wall. The 24×36 poster at 111 DPI will look soft up close, though it may pass at a distance.

Verdicts used here

  • Excellent (300+ DPI): sharp even when examined closely.
  • Very good (240–299): nearly indistinguishable from 300 on most printers.
  • Fair (150–239): good for wall prints viewed from a few feet; slightly soft up close.
  • Poor (under 150): visible softness or pixelation; only for very large prints seen from far away.

Aspect ratio and cropping

Most phones shoot 4:3 and most cameras 3:2, but print sizes vary: 4×6 and 24×36 are 3:2, 8×10 and 16×20 are 5:4, 5×7 is 7:5 and 11×14 is about 14:11. When the shapes differ, filling the paper means trimming part of the image. A 3:2 camera photo printed at 8×10 loses about 17% of its width. Check the crop preview when ordering, or choose "fit" with a white border to keep the whole frame.

Tips for better prints

  • Use the original file. Messaging apps and social media shrink photos; a file from a chat app may be a fraction of the camera's pixels.
  • Crop before you check. If you've cropped in an editor, enter the cropped pixel size, not the camera's.
  • Don't just change the DPI field. Setting 300 DPI in an editor without resampling changes nothing about the pixels; only the pixel count matters.
  • Test before going big. Order a small section print or a proof before an expensive canvas or metal print.
