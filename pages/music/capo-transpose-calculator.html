How transposing works

Transposing moves every note of a song by the same distance, so the melody and harmony keep their shape but sit higher or lower. For chord charts that means shifting each chord's root, and the bass note of any slash chord, by the same number of semitones. The chord quality, like m, 7, maj7, sus4 or add9, stays exactly the same.

A capo does the transposing for you on guitar: every fret raises everything by one semitone. That's why players use a capo to reach a singer's key while keeping familiar open chord shapes.

The formula

semitone numbers: C=0, C#/Db=1, D=2, D#/Eb=3, E=4, F=5, F#/Gb=6, G=7, G#/Ab=8, A=9, A#/Bb=10, B=11
new root = (old root + semitones) mod 12
capo fret = (song key − shape key) mod 12
for a minor key, use its relative major (3 semitones up)

Example: transposing up a whole step

The chords G D/F# Em C moved up 2 semitones, spelled with sharps because the new key, A major, uses sharps:

OriginalSemitone mathTransposed
G7 + 2 = 9A
D/F#2 + 2 = 4, bass 6 + 2 = 8E/G#
Em4 + 2 = 6F#m
C0 + 2 = 2D

A guitarist could instead keep the original shapes and put a capo on fret 2.

Example: finding a capo fret

A song written as Bb F/A Gm Eb is in Bb major (10). To play it with G shapes (7): (10 − 7) mod 12 = 3, so capo on fret 3 and play G D/F# Em C. The other options are A shapes at capo 1, E shapes at capo 6, D shapes at capo 8 and C shapes at capo 10.

Tips

  • Find the singer's key first. Try the chorus's highest note; if it strains, transpose down 1 or 2 semitones and try again.
  • G and C shapes sound full and ring well with a capo; E and A shapes keep the bass deep for lower keys.
  • Two guitars? Have one play open shapes and the other the same chords with a capo and different shapes (say G shapes open and D shapes at capo 5) for a wider sound.
  • Put the home chord first when using auto spelling or auto key, since the first chord sets the key.
